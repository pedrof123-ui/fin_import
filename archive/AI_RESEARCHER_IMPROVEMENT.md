2) Depending on the company, use of one or two of the following academic frameworks:

    1. Porter’s Five ForcesDeveloped by Michael Porter at Harvard Business Review, this framework assesses the structural attractiveness and long-term profitability of an industry. For an equity analyst, it directly impacts your operating margin assumptions by identifying where pricing pressure might compress profitability.Threat of New Entrants: High barriers to entry (e.g., massive CapEx, regulatory hurdles) protect incumbent market share.Bargaining Power of Suppliers: If suppliers are concentrated, they can squeeze the company’s gross margins.Bargaining Power of Buyers: High buyer power limits a company's pricing power, forcing discount cycles.Threat of Substitutes: Look for technological or structural shifts that render the company's product obsolete.Competitive Rivalry: Intense price competition inside the industry leads to margin erosion.

    2. Resource-Based View (RBV) & VRIO FrameworkPopularized by Jay Barney, the Resource-Based View looks inside the firm to see if its internal assets (tangible or intangible) can sustain a competitive advantage. The VRIO matrix acts as an excellent sanity check for your terminal growth rate ($g$) and return on invested capital (ROIC) assumptions:AttributeQuestion for the AnalystValuation ImpactValuable?Does the resource exploit an opportunity or neutralize a threat?Drives short-term revenue/cost efficiency.Rare?Is the asset controlled by only a few competing firms?Allows for premium pricing.Inimitably Costly?Is it highly expensive or difficult for competitors to duplicate?Protects the duration of high ROIC.Organized?Is the company organized to capture the value of this asset?Determines execution risk and efficiency.

    3. The Ansoff Matrix (Product/Market Expansion Grid)Created by Igor Ansoff, this framework evaluates a company's growth vectors. In an equity report, this is highly effective for breaking down top-line revenue growth forecasts into specific risk-adjusted tranches.Market Penetration (Existing Product, Existing Market): Low-risk growth. Driven by volume or market share capture.Product Development (New Product, Existing Market): Cross-selling new innovations to an established customer base (e.g., Apple launching a new service). Requires R&D tracking.Market Development (Existing Product, New Market): Geographic expansion or targeting a new demographic. Assess localized regulatory and distribution hurdles.Diversification (New Product, New Market): High risk. Entering an entirely new ecosystem (often via M&A). Requires intense scrutiny regarding integration risk and synergy assumptions.

    4. Blue Ocean StrategyDeveloped by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, this framework looks at whether a company is competing in an overcrowded market ("Red Ocean") or creating uncontested market space ("Blue Ocean").Strategic Application: Excellent for analyzing high-growth disruptors or tech firms.Valuation Impact: Companies successfully executing a Blue Ocean strategy often command temporary monopolies, justifying higher multiple expansions (P/E, EV/EBITDA) and explosive early-stage revenue growth. The analyst must evaluate how long the company can maintain the "ocean" before fast-followers turn it red.
